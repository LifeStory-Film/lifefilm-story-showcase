# Wedding Videographer at Saddlerock Ranch — Vineyard Ceremony and Oak Grove Coverage

A wedding videographer at Saddlerock Ranch works with 3 distinct scenes: the vineyard ceremony in late-afternoon light, the oak grove reception after sunset, and the alpaca pasture portraits between the two. Saddlerock Ranch sits in the Santa Monica Mountains above Malibu, and the property's 32 acres of terraced vineyards, oak canopy, and working ranch animals create a rustic-editorial environment that requires a 2-shooter video team and 10 hours of coverage to capture completely.

The vineyard terrace ceremony site faces west toward the ocean ridge, which means the best light arrives between 4:00 PM and 5:30 PM from late spring through early fall. Couples who book the ceremony for 5:00 PM get golden-hour vows footage; couples who push to 6:00 PM risk losing detail in the wide shots as the sun drops behind the ridge. The videographer's first decision at Saddlerock Ranch is whether to prioritize the ceremony light or the cocktail-hour pasture light — you cannot have both at their peak with a single timeline.

## Why Saddlerock Ranch Films Differently Than a Coastal Venue

![Group of cattle standing behind a wire fence in a sunny farm pasture.](/blog-images/wedding-videographer-saddlerock-ranch/section-1-3915e736afa1.jpg)

*Photo by vee terzy on Pexels*


Saddlerock Ranch is a working vineyard and exotic animal ranch, not a hotel or country club. The ceremony happens on an open terrace with no shade structure, the cocktail hour moves through the alpaca and zebra pastures, and the reception is set under centuries-old oak trees with string lights but no permanent pavilion. This creates three cinematography challenges that don't exist at venues like Bel-Air Bay Club or Resort at Pelican Hill.

First, the light changes abruptly between spaces. The vineyard terrace is full sun until the ridge shadow cuts across at 6:15 PM. The oak grove is dappled shade all day, then near-dark by 7:30 PM. A videographer shooting both locations on the same day needs to plan for a 4-stop exposure swing between the ceremony and the reception entrance, which is why we always bring a second shooter with a separate rig optimized for low light.

Second, the ranch animals are part of the coverage. Couples book Saddlerock Ranch specifically for the alpaca pasture portraits and the possibility of a giraffe sighting during cocktail hour. A wedding film that omits those moments feels generic, like it could have been shot anywhere. We allocate 20 minutes of the timeline between ceremony and reception exclusively for pasture coverage — the couple walking with the alpacas, the reaction shots when guests notice the zebras, the quiet portrait moment under the oak at the edge of the vineyard.

Third, the property has no indoor backup plan. If it rains, the ceremony moves under the oak grove and the reception shifts to the adjacent covered terrace, but both are open-air. The videographer's gear needs to be weather-sealed, and the coverage plan needs to account for the fact that oak-grove ceremony light is fundamentally different from vineyard-terrace light — softer, greener, harder to backlight for a cinematic wide shot.

## Scene 1: Vineyard Ceremony Coverage at Saddlerock Ranch

![Bride and groom walking hand in hand through a vineyard during a sunny fall day.](/blog-images/wedding-videographer-saddlerock-ranch/section-2-ff85c1487ac4.jpg)

*Photo by Photography Maghradze PH on Pexels*


The vineyard ceremony site is a stone terrace carved into the hillside, with guest seating on the terrace and the couple standing at the western edge with the ocean ridge behind them. From a videography perspective, this is a **backlit ceremony** — the couple is silhouetted against the sky, which creates a cinematic wide shot but makes it difficult to capture facial detail during the vows without a second angle.

Our standard Saddlerock Ranch ceremony setup uses 2 cameras: one wide-angle lock-off at the back of the guest seating (capturing the full terrace, the couple, and the ridge skyline), and one handheld shooter positioned to the side of the aisle with a 70–200mm lens for close vow coverage and parent reaction shots. The handheld shooter moves during the processional to catch the bride's entrance from the stone staircase, then repositions for the vows.

The processional is the only part of the ceremony where the videographer has access to the aisle. Once the couple is at the altar, the shooter stays to the side to avoid blocking guest sightlines. Planners at Saddlerock Ranch are strict about this — the property is small enough that a videographer standing in the aisle is visible to everyone, and it breaks the editorial look that luxury couples expect.

Vow audio is captured with 2 wireless lavalier mics — one on the groom, one on the officiant. The vineyard terrace is open to wind, and without dual audio, half the vow coverage is unusable. We also run a backup recorder on the DJ's soundboard when amplified vows are part of the ceremony, but the primary audio always comes from the lav mics.

The ceremony typically runs 25 minutes. The videographer's shot list includes: bride's entrance (stone staircase), parents' reactions during the processional, full vows (wide and close), ring exchange, first kiss, and recessional. The recessional is the second backlit moment — the couple walks back down the aisle directly into the golden-hour sun, which creates a lens-flare editorial shot if the handheld shooter is positioned correctly at the base of the staircase.

## Scene 2: Alpaca Pasture Portraits and Cocktail Hour

![Charming brown alpacas relaxing outdoors on a sunny farm day.](/blog-images/wedding-videographer-saddlerock-ranch/section-3-87d4fed05a12.jpg)

*Photo by Meraliential on Pexels*


Immediately after the ceremony, the couple has a 20-minute window for portraits before cocktail hour begins. At Saddlerock Ranch, this window is spent in the alpaca pasture, not at the vineyard edge. The pasture sits below the oak grove on the eastern side of the property, and the light there at 5:30 PM is open shade with a warm bounce from the hillside — softer and more flattering than the direct vineyard sun.

The alpacas are genuinely part of the portrait session. They approach the couple, they photobomb the wide shots, and occasionally they spit. A videographer who has not shot at Saddlerock Ranch before will try to control this; a videographer who knows the venue leans into it. The best portrait footage from Saddlerock Ranch is always the moment when the bride laughs at an alpaca chewing her veil, not the posed two-shot at the vineyard gate.

We shoot the pasture portraits as a mix of static compositions (couple walking toward camera with alpacas in the midground) and handheld follow shots (couple moving through the pasture, reacting to the animals). The follow shots require a gimbal or shoulder rig to stay stable on uneven terrain. Saddlerock Ranch is a working ranch, and the pasture ground is dirt, grass, and occasional mud depending on the season.

Cocktail hour happens simultaneously on the upper terrace near the oak grove. Guests circulate between the bar, the lawn games, and the animal pastures. The second shooter covers cocktail hour while the primary shooter finishes portraits, then both shooters converge on the pasture for a final 5-minute couple session before the reception entrance.

The giraffe pen is adjacent to the cocktail terrace, and if the giraffes are visible during cocktail hour, we capture guest reactions. Not every Saddlerock Ranch wedding includes giraffe footage — the animals are not always in the pen — but when they are, the footage becomes a signature moment in the highlight reel.

## Scene 3: Oak Grove Reception and String-Light Coverage

![Warm and cozy string lights illuminating an outdoor garden setting at dusk, creating a festive ambiance.](/blog-images/wedding-videographer-saddlerock-ranch/section-4-d7566f869970.jpg)

*Photo by Michael Morse on Pexels*


The reception is set under the oak grove on the northern side of the property. Long farm tables are arranged under the canopy, with string lights overhead and votive candles on the tables. By the time the reception begins at 7:00 PM, the natural light is gone, and the entire scene is lit by string lights, candles, and occasional uplighting on the oak trunks.

This is where the second shooter with a low-light rig becomes essential. Our reception setup uses a Sony A7S III (or equivalent) with a fast prime lens (35mm f/1.4 or 50mm f/1.2) to capture the ambient string-light environment without adding video lights. Video lights destroy the editorial look at Saddlerock Ranch — the whole point of the oak grove is the warm, intimate glow from the string lights, and adding a harsh LED panel flattens that entirely.

The reception entrance happens at the top of the stone staircase that descends into the oak grove. The couple is introduced, they walk down the staircase together, and they enter the reception space to applause. The videographer positions at the base of the staircase to capture the descent with the string-lit oak canopy in the background.

First dance, parent dances, toasts, and dinner happen under the oaks. The videographer moves between static tripod setups (wide shot of the entire reception space during toasts) and handheld gimbal shots (close follow during the first dance, table-to-table coverage during dinner). The oak grove acoustics are good — sound does not echo or get lost the way it does in a ballroom — so toast audio is clean even without a dedicated PA system.

Cake cutting and bouquet toss (if applicable) happen on the adjacent lawn, where there is slightly more light. The final coverage moment is the last dance under the string lights, typically around 10:00 PM. By that point, the reception has been running for 3 hours, and the videographer has shot approximately 6 hours of total coverage counting ceremony prep, ceremony, portraits, cocktail hour, and reception.

## Coverage Timeline: Ceremony Through Reception at Saddlerock Ranch

A typical Saddlerock Ranch wedding timeline from a videographer's perspective:

1. **3:00 PM** — Video team arrives. Establish ceremony-site lock-off camera, test audio, scout pasture and oak grove for backup angles.
2. **4:00 PM** — Couple getting-ready coverage (if included in package). Otherwise, team preps reception lighting tests and confirms string-light exposure settings.
3. **5:00 PM** — Ceremony begins. 2-camera setup: wide lock-off + handheld aisle/vow coverage. Processional, vows, ring exchange, first kiss, recessional. 25 minutes total.
4. **5:30 PM** — Pasture portraits. Couple + alpacas, 20 minutes. Primary shooter on portraits, second shooter captures cocktail-hour guest coverage on upper terrace.
5. **6:00 PM** — Cocktail hour concludes. Final 5-minute couple portraits at vineyard edge (golden-hour backlight).
6. **6:30 PM** — Reception entrance. Couple introduced at stone staircase, descends into oak grove.
7. **6:45 PM** — First dance under string lights. Low-light handheld gimbal coverage.
8. **7:00 PM** — Parent dances, toasts, dinner. Mix of wide tripod shots (toasts) and roaming handheld (dinner table reactions).
9. **8:30 PM** — Cake cutting on adjacent lawn.
10. **9:00 PM–10:00 PM** — Open dancing under oaks. Capture 10–15 minutes of dance-floor coverage, prioritizing the last dance.
11. **10:00 PM** — Wrap. Total coverage: 7 hours (ceremony through last dance).

This timeline assumes a 5:00 PM ceremony start. Couples who start earlier (4:00 PM ceremony) gain an extra hour of daylight for portraits but lose the golden-hour vow light. Couples who start later (6:00 PM ceremony) get sunset vows but have no daylight left for pasture portraits, which shifts those portraits to the pre-ceremony window and compresses the getting-ready coverage.

The property is large enough that the ceremony site, pasture, and oak grove are not visible from a single position, and the key moments (vows, alpaca portraits, reception entrance, first dance) happen in different locations with different light conditions.

A 2-shooter team allows us to cover the ceremony with both a wide cinematic angle and close vow/reaction detail. It allows us to run a static tripod during toasts while the second shooter moves handheld for reaction close-ups.

Single-shooter coverage at Saddlerock Ranch typically results in a wedding film that feels incomplete — either the ceremony is well-covered but the pasture portraits are missing, or the portraits are there but the reception entrance was not captured because the shooter was still in the pasture. ## Weather Contingency and Coverage Adjustments

Saddlerock Ranch has no enclosed indoor space. If it rains, the ceremony moves from the vineyard terrace to the covered oak grove terrace, and the reception shifts to the adjacent covered pavilion. Both backup locations are open-air, so light rain is still a factor, and the videographer's gear must be weather-sealed.

The oak grove ceremony backup changes the light and framing significantly. The vineyard terrace ceremony is backlit and open; the oak grove ceremony is shaded and more intimate. The wide cinematic shot that works on the vineyard terrace does not work under the oaks — the frame needs to be tighter, the lens needs to be faster, and the ISO needs to be higher to compensate for the 3-stop light loss.

We always scout both ceremony locations during the pre-ceremony site walk, even if the weather forecast is clear, so that if the ceremony moves at the last minute, we already know where to position the cameras and how to adjust exposure. Couples booking Saddlerock Ranch should ask their videographer whether the team has shot the venue in both configurations — a videographer who has only covered sunny-day Saddlerock Ranch weddings will struggle if the ceremony moves to the oak grove.
